The Origin of Self-Consciousness from the Lowdown on My Grandfather’s Wig

Written by Peter Mangiaracina

Everybody always asks me why I am so fanatical about not losing my hair. After all, there are plenty of men my age who are completely bald. They seem to live with it just fine. Some even shave their heads and women think it’s sexy. Go figure. However, for me, the mere thought of losing my hair is as serious as having a heart attack.

There is good reason for me to worry. Every man in my extended family is bald except my father, whose hair is nothing like mine, and my cousin, who probably got his hair genes from his mother’s side of the family. Those Cromagnons even grew hair between their shoulder blades. My cousin’s father, my mother’s brother, went bald at 40. All my other cousins are bald. My brother is bald, and he’s seven years younger than I am. All my uncles were bald, and both my grandfathers, too. Every morning I stand in front of the mirror for a long time, wrangling my hair into a semblance of normalcy. The time you spend grooming you hair grows in inverse relationship to the amount of hair you have.

I first noticed an almost imperceptible speck of missing hair in a photo taken from behind while I was painting a fence. I was just 30 years old. After my initial red-alert dread, I deceived myself that it was a sleight of the light, nothing more. I decided from that moment until this very day not to let the barber show me the customary back-of-the-head mirror view after a haircut. Even if I forget to tell him not to, I’ll focus on the tiny snipped-off hairs sprinkled on the front of my apron, nod, and congratulate him on a job well done. “Oh, yeah. Good job. Can I get up and leave now?”

However, I paid a steep price for ignoring the insidious horror that was playing out back there. When I reached 50, that fleck became a wide and sparse forest through which, contrary to the old saying, I could see each of the trees.

That’s when I started using Minoxidil, otherwise known as Rogaine. I slathered it on morning and night for ten years. It didn’t seem to slow my hair loss, and after using it my hair looked like the glaze on a donut. Finally, I consulted a dermatologist who did a test. He told me that Minoxidil is completely useless for my particular follicular configuration, so I had spent ten years wasting money on an expensive and fruitless, hair-saving endeavor.

This dermatologist prescribed Finesteride pills, a drug commonly used for prostate hyperplasia, but in small doses it’s supposed to grow hair. It didn’t; in fact, I had to stop taking it because somehow it was affecting — or at least according to my urologist suspected of affecting — my testosterone levels. And we can’t have that, can we?

The dermatologist then prescribed Finestopic. It is the same drug, but sprayed on your head. It dries quickly thereby avoiding the whole donut glaze hair fiasco. I’m not completely sure, but I think it’s working. With the help of some judiciously applied keratin hair fibers sprinkled back there, the gap is no longer visible.

I can trace the reason I am perpetually panicky about my hair to my grandfather, an old-school Sicilian. He was a carpenter; nevertheless, he dressed in a suit and tie for work every day. At work he would take off the suit jacket and carefully hang it up, roll up his sleeves, grab his tools, and get to work. However, he always wore a fedora. This little gray fedora sat on his head like a stubborn bottle cap no matter the hour of the day or the day of the week, no matter at work or at home. No one ever saw him without this fedora. You see, he had gone bald when he was in his 30s and was humiliated by his naked head and the little fringe surrounding it at ear level.

When he was in his 50s, he bought himself a wig. I can’t dignify it by calling it a toupee; it was more akin to a cheap welcome mat. Nevertheless, what it did for my grandfather‘s confidence was worth the two or three dollars he must have paid for it.

Now it wasn’t necessary for him to wear the fedora all the time. He would brazenly take off his fedora with a flourish when he arrived at work, or upon visiting friends for dinner.

The wig was synthetic and jet black. However, the fringe around my grandfather’s head was completely white. The whole effect was kind of like the inverse stripe of a skunk with the black in the middle and the white on the sides. Nevertheless, he was very proud of this wig and would never take it off, never, not even in front of my step grandmother. When it was time to wash it, he would skulk into the bathroom, lock the door, and carefully and affectionately remove the wig from his head to wash it and dry it thoroughly before putting it back on again and leaving the bathroom.

His dignity was so wrapped up in that wig that it killed him.

One day, in 1977 I believe, he’d just gotten home from work. He was 65 and still working. During dinner, he started feeling mild chest pains. My uncle immediately took him to the hospital, fearing he was having a heart attack. They waited in the waiting room for 45 minutes, but the pain didn’t get any worse, and was in fact starting to disappear. Everyone breathed a sigh of relief, but decided he should stay and have a workup done.

At that moment, an orderly came up to my grandfather and my uncle and requested that they follow him to an examination room. My grandfather was placed on a gurney. The orderly politely asked my grandfather to remove his wig. My grandfather politely refused. The orderly explained that in order to run the tests it was necessary to remove the wig. My grandfather refused again, but not so politely. When my grandfather refused the second time, the orderly reached for the wig to remove it. This enraged my grandfather, he rose from the gurney and promptly punched the orderly in the nose. The orderly’s nose began to bleed profusely. He yelled for several other colleagues to assist him in restraining my grandfather. A fight ensued between this old man and several hulking orderlies, with my uncle standing by amazed and confused.

During this fight, my grandfather clutched his chest and keeled over. He was dead of a massive heart attack within 15 minutes. You could say he died for his self-respect, and that self respect was intimately associated with a cheap wig and the fear that someone would see him without it.

My father recounted this story to me several times during my childhood while running his hands through his hair and thanking the Lord he got his hair from his mother’s side of the family. For him and his father before him, hair loss was the same as losing one’s manhood. Although I am an enlightened soul, and I am well aware that manhood cannot be reduced to the hair on your head, there are fears so primal, so ingrained in you when you’re young, that logic and sensibility are no defense against the feelings they engender.

So, go ahead. Ask me why it is so important for me not to lose my hair. I’ll tell you the story again.
