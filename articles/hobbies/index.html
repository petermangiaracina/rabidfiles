Hobbies

One way to destress is to take up a hobby. Hobbies promote a flow state which can reduce anxiety, boost your mood, and even slow your heart rate.

Written by Peter Mangiaracina

I work a lot with doctors. Doctors dedicate their lives and practically all of their time taking care of people and saving their lives. Most of the doctors I know, and I teach a lot of doctors, have physical difficulties. They have chronic pain in their necks and their backs, especially surgeons. Some of them drink too much. I’m not saying they are alcoholics, but a bottle of wine every day is probably crossing the line a little. Many have families and their free time is dedicated to activities with them. They don’t think much about themselves.

I always try to counsel them about how to simplify their lives; I can see when someone is suffering. I tell them to get a hobby. Hobbies have the advantage of putting you into a flow state, a state in which time disappears and the mind rests in a trance, completely absorbed with an activity that has nothing to do with your job. It reduces anxiety, boosts your mood, and even slows your heart rate. Yeah, okay, meditation does the same thing, but try getting a busy person to sit still and do nothing but breathe as a solution to stress. Many just don’t believe it will help, or feel it’s a waste of time. It’s not, but let’s save that discussion for another time.

Taking up a hobby might be a little bit more palatable to someone who is suspicious of a practice which asks you to “empty” your mind. However, a lot of people say they just don’t have time to do anything else, but the cost of not doing anything to alleviate stress is a prescription for an early heart attack. You have time. Everyone has time. It’s more question of how you parcel out your time rather than not having any time at all. When people tell me they don't have time, it's an excuse not to start something new. When you get into a rhythm of work-family, work-family, there’s a lot of resistance to inserting something new into the routine. Again, a prescription for physical manifestations of a stressed mind.

I get up at 5 o'clock in the morning and frequently work until 9 PM or 9:30 PM at least four days a week. I have been doing that for more than 35 years. If I didn't destress from time to time, I would be a basket case by now. I have survived these many years without passing a threshold of malignant stress because I have many hobbies. I make videos, take photos, post-process photos and videos, I write, I read a lot, I practice my guitar, compose music, and I'm always trying some new application or figuring out how to work a new device. I even do many of the things that I mentioned in service of my work. No, I'm not a Renaissance man, I am more of a jack of all trades and master of none kind of guy, but I enjoy myself and that's the point: I'm happy.

You’ve probably heard of flow — it’s the state you get in when you’re completely absorbed in something. Have you ever been working on a project and completely lost all sense of self and time? That’s flow. It reduces anxiety, boosts your mood, and even slows your heart rate.

It’s not just being in flow that helps your happiness. Repetitive creative motions like knitting, drawing, or writing help activate flow, and are all tasks that create a result. And when you succeed at creating a result, no matter what it is, your brain is flooded with dopamine, that feel-good chemical that actually helps motivate you. Whether or not you’re aware of your increased happiness, the hit of dopamine you get after being in flow will drive and influence you toward similar behavior.
